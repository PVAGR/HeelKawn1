# HeelKawn — Visual Direction

**Status:** Direction, not locked. The canon deliberately leaves the final art language and exact visual scale open; this document is a working expression of that direction, not a decree.

## The one idea the world must communicate

This place existed before I arrived. Everyone here has a life. History is happening whether I participate or not.

That is the visual expression of HeelKawn's core canon: a persistent autonomous world of individually minded people whose unscripted lives create families, settlements, civilizations, wars, knowledge, myths, ruins, and history.

## What HeelKawn should look like

Imagine standing on a mountain.

Below you is not a "map."

It is an actual living country.

A river twists through a valley because of the terrain, not because somebody needed a decorative river. A dirt road follows the easiest route around hills. The road becomes wider near a town because generations of people have traveled it. Farms spread outward from homes. Smoke rises from chimneys. A watermill sits where someone found usable current. A ruined watchtower sits on a ridge because another civilization built it 170 years ago.

Farther away, another settlement is barely visible through the trees.

Beyond that are mountains.

Behind those mountains may be another people, another language, another government, another religion, and perhaps the HeelKawnians in the first valley don't even know they exist.

That is HeelKawn.

## The visual formula

- **Close up:** Kingdom Come Deliverance + Valheim + Vintage Story + Kenshi.
- **Village/town scale:** Ostriv + Foundation + RimWorld + The Guild + Bellwright.
- **City/civilization scale:** Songs of Syx.
- **Kingdom/continental scale:** Crusader Kings + WorldBox + Ages of Conflict.
- **War:** Bannerlord + Total War + Dynasty Warriors, while remembering every visible soldier is still an actual HeelKawnian.
- **Exploration/discovery:** Caves of Qud + Kenshi + Skyrim + No Man's Sky philosophy.

The source material consistently describes exactly this kind of multi-scale world rather than one genre operating alone.

# 1. The terrain should dominate the world

Do not begin by filling the map with cities.

HeelKawn should initially feel enormous, wild, and underpopulated.

There should be:

- huge forests
- mountain ranges
- valleys
- plains
- rivers that actually divide regions
- lakes
- wetlands
- coastlines
- islands
- caves
- passes through mountains
- natural harbors
- fertile river valleys
- harsh areas where settlement is difficult
- places people simply have not explored yet

A river shouldn't just be blue decoration. It affects travel, drinking, farming, fishing, bridges, settlement placement, trade, military movement, borders, floods, mills, and eventually history.

A mountain pass might become one of the most important places on the continent because it is the only practical route between two civilizations.

That is how geography becomes politics.

---

# 2. Settlements should look grown, not placed

This is extremely important.

Do not build: house, house, house, blacksmith, castle, wall.

HeelKawn settlements should look like people slowly occupied a place.

### Early settlement

Maybe there is only a campfire, several crude shelters, a storage area, chopped trees, paths created by walking, cooking space, water access, and little gardens. Nothing looks symmetrical.

### Village

Years later: houses cluster around families. Footpaths connect them. A smith lives near where fuel and ore are accessible. Farmers build near good soil. Livestock changes the ground. A gathering place forms. Someone builds a shrine. A road begins connecting the village to another community.

### Town

Now you begin seeing workshops, markets, wells, bridges, mills, larger homes, inns, warehouses, religious buildings, roads, walls in dangerous regions, gates, administration, burial grounds, schools, guild spaces.

### City

Only much later should you see districts, major markets, stone roads, defenses, towers, palaces or council halls, libraries, monuments, dense housing, industrial areas, ports, temples, slums, estates, military quarters, and suburbs.

But these should exist because people built them over generations.

The canon explicitly says settlement formation should emerge from continued concentration of people, shelter, resources, cooperation and memory rather than being a scripted "city spawned" event.

---

# 3. Buildings need visible history

This might become one of HeelKawn's strongest visual features.

A house shouldn't forever look freshly spawned.

It might have: new wood → weathered wood → repairs → additions → different roofing → fire damage → replacement stone foundation → abandonment → vegetation → ruin.

A castle might contain a wooden fort underneath its later stone construction.

A city wall might have visibly patched sections where an ancient siege broke it.

An old road may disappear into forest.

A ruined town might later become a new neighborhood.

HeelKawn's canon treats land as containing memory, prior use, graves, boundaries, claims and future needs rather than blank real estate.

So the environment itself should eventually function like an archaeological record.

---

# 4. Use Crusader Kings borders — but only when zoomed out

The kingdom layer should look gorgeous.

At great distance, terrain remains visible underneath political information. You see:

- kingdom name
- colored border
- capital
- major settlements
- rivers
- mountains
- roads
- trade routes
- perhaps disputed territory

Something like Crusader Kings.

But the colored country should not literally exist on the ground.

Zoom closer: border color fades. Now you see actual forts, roads, patrols, farms and settlements.

Zoom all the way into the forest where two kingdoms supposedly meet? There might be nothing there at all except trees.

A border exists because people claim, recognize, patrol or fight over it.

The canon explicitly treats ownership and authority as social realities that must be recognized, defended or remembered rather than magical UI facts.

---

# 5. The zoom system could define the entire game

### LEVEL 1 — WORLD

Like WorldBox/CK. You see continents, civilizations, major migrations and political boundaries.

### LEVEL 2 — REGION

You see roads, villages, armies, forests, rivers, farms and local territory.

### LEVEL 3 — SETTLEMENT

Songs of Syx/Foundation. You can watch hundreds of people living.

### LEVEL 4 — STREET

Kingdom Come/RimWorld life expressed in 3D. You see: a smith hammering metal, a child following a parent, someone carrying grain, two people arguing, builders repairing a roof, guards changing watch, a farmer bringing produce home.

### LEVEL 5 — PERSON

Then you become one HeelKawnian. Third person, possibly optional first person.

Now the enormous political map above you disappears. You know only what this person knows.

That matters because HeelKawn canon specifically says ordinary people do not receive omniscient world knowledge and that fog of knowledge is more important than fog of war.

Observer mode, by contrast, can follow a person, family, settlement, artifact or civilization across history.

---

# 6. HeelKawnians should make the world visually alive

If you stand in a settlement for ten minutes, you should see lives happening without you.

Not generic NPCs pacing between waypoints.

You should see:

- someone gathering firewood
- someone repairing their home
- someone teaching another person
- children nearby
- someone traveling out of town
- a trader entering
- someone hauling stone
- a carpenter working
- someone preparing food
- someone resting
- someone visiting family
- someone carrying water
- someone building
- someone praying
- someone training
- someone arguing
- someone leaving permanently
- someone arriving and trying to fit in
- someone doing absolutely nothing because they are exhausted

Every HeelKawnian is supposed to be the protagonist of their own life, not scenery around the player.

That means when you look into a house at night and see a candle burning, you should eventually be able to think: someone actually lives there.

The canon literally uses that as part of HeelKawn's final success test: a distant light should represent someone with their own memory, fear, duty and plan.

---

# 7. Architecture should evolve locally

Do not make one global HeelKawn building set.

A forest culture could build heavy timber, moss roofs, carved wood, compact settlements.

A mountain civilization: stone, defensive terraces, narrow roads, mines.

A river civilization: wooden walkways, bridges, mills, warehouses, canals.

A wealthy empire: stone roads, public buildings, monuments, aqueducts.

A nomadic people: portable structures, animal infrastructure, temporary markets.

And architecture should change because of: available material, climate, knowledge, wealth, religion, defense, trade, local builders, past disasters.

Eventually you should be able to look at a building and have some idea who built it and when.

---

# 8. Do not make the fantasy oversaturated

Make the baseline world feel grounded and believable first.

Grass. Mud. Timber. Stone. Rain. Snow. Firelight. Fog. Dust. Smoke. Sunset. Moonlight.

Then let fantasy become striking because it is uncommon.

A strange ruin deep in a forest becomes much more powerful if every house isn't glowing purple.

A genuinely magical site should feel different from normal land.

A legendary artifact should look extraordinary because normal smiths make normal swords.

HeelKawn's metaphysical layer is supposed to remain mysterious and interpreted rather than turn into a visible RPG morality/spell meter.

---

# 9. War should visually transform civilization

Before war: farmers working, merchants traveling, construction, children, markets.

Then mobilization begins. Messengers move. Supplies accumulate. Smiths produce equipment. People leave home. Roads fill with soldiers. Wagons follow. Families remain behind.

Then armies meet.

And when war ends, the world doesn't reset. There are graves, burned homes, abandoned farms, widows and children, missing workers, damaged roads, captured equipment, new borders, ruined walls, battlefield debris, stories about what happened.

Because in HeelKawn, every soldier remains a person and households pay the cost of war.

---

# 10. The world needs quiet

This is crucial.

Do not constantly throw content at the player.

You should be able to spend an hour farming, fishing, cutting timber, building a roof, walking to another village, trading, sitting at a tavern, watching rain, teaching a child a craft.

The material repeatedly says HeelKawn can experience decades where nothing "epic" happens.

That is necessary. Because if everything is legendary, nothing becomes legendary.

---

# The visual style

Not photorealism. Not classic blocky stylized-by-default.

**Stylized grounded low-poly realism:**

- realistic proportions
- beautiful terrain
- strong silhouettes
- moderately detailed modular buildings
- natural colors
- excellent lighting
- dense foliage where appropriate
- good weather
- atmospheric fog
- high-quality materials
- characters readable from a distance

This gives you a fantasy world that can be beautiful without murdering performance.

The screenshots should eventually be able to look like a tiny medieval world from above and then, seconds later, an RPG someone could actually live inside.

# The image

You spawn as observer over a green continent at sunrise. There are no giant UI buttons covering it.

You see three settlements separated by kilometers of wilderness. One settlement has perhaps forty people. Another has six houses. The third is only a camp. A river runs between them.

You zoom downward. Political overlays disappear. You pass over a road. There is a traveler on it. You follow him. He reaches town just as rain begins. People move under roofs. A builder stops working. Smoke rises from a hearth. A child runs inside. A farmer continues trying to finish unloading a cart.

Across town stands an old stone foundation nobody alive remembers building. You click it. The observer chronicle reveals that a house burned there ninety-two years earlier. Someone living nearby believes a completely different story about what happened.

Then you zoom back out. Far to the north, a new group has just crossed a mountain range. Nobody in this village knows they exist yet.

That is HeelKawn.

A world where the visible landscape is the accumulated physical evidence of millions of individual lives.

---

## Build order

Build that feeling first — terrain, one living settlement, seamless observer→person scale, and organic construction — before worrying about the entire planet.

## Notes on canon alignment

- Settlement formation must emerge from concentration of people, shelter, resources, cooperation and memory — never a scripted city-spawn event (matches kernel rule: no fake systems, facts first).
- Ownership/authority are social realities — recognized, defended or remembered — not magical UI facts.
- Fog of knowledge matters more than fog of war; ordinary people do not receive omniscient world knowledge.
- The metaphysical layer stays mysterious and interpreted, never a visible morality/spell meter.
- Geometry/terrain is not blank real estate: land carries memory, prior use, graves, boundaries, claims, and future needs.
